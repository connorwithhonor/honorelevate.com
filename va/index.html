Before we talk attendance

Nine lenders got hit in one sweep. Here are three of them.

In 2020 the CFPB ran a sweep of mortgage companies advertising VA-guaranteed loans, opened after the VA itself flagged the problem. Nine consent orders came out of it. The findings were the same each time: mailers with false, misleading or inaccurate statements, misrepresented credit terms, and missing Regulation Z disclosures. Nobody pitching you a webinar opens here, which is why I am opening here.

Low VA Rates

$1,800,000 civil penalty, October 2020. The ninth and last case in the sweep. Sent 1,141,690 mailers built around a 1.75% rate framing.

Service 1st Mortgage

$230,000 civil penalty. Seventh case in the same sweep. CFPA deceptive acts, MAP Rule, and Regulation Z.

Accelerate Mortgage

$225,000 civil penalty, September 2020. Mailers that misrepresented credit terms and omitted required disclosures.

The MAP Rule and Regulation N are the same rule, and it requires you to keep every materially different commercial communication for 24 months from the last date you sent it. Failing to keep those records is itself a violation, separate from anything you said in the ad. Every landing page, text and email version this system sends is archived automatically, timestamped, with a copy of exactly what the consumer saw. That is not a marketing feature. That is insurance against the sweep that took those nine down.

Filling the room

Two engines, and one rule you cannot target your way around

A webinar with nobody in it is a rehearsal. Traffic is its own build, and on paid it runs into a wall most people do not find out about until their ads underperform and nobody can explain why.

A VA loan ad is classified by Meta as both housing and credit. That puts it in the Special Ad Category, where you cannot target by ZIP code, cannot target by age, cannot target by gender, and cannot use Lookalike Audiences at all. Location is a 15-mile radius minimum. As of 2026 Meta's classifiers apply this automatically off your imagery, so a loan calculator or a for-sale sign in the creative triggers it whether you selected the category or not.
01

Paid, built for blunt reach

No lookalikes and no age filter means the audience arrives wide. So the creative and the offer do the qualifying that targeting is not allowed to do. Ads are written to make the wrong person scroll past and the right person stop.

02

Organic, built for proof

Short video answering the questions veterans actually asked on the last webinar. No ad account, no category restriction, and it compounds. This is where the trust gets built that paid traffic cashes in.

03

Your own list, the cheapest lane

Custom audiences built from your own borrower list are still permitted inside a single business portfolio. That is the IRRRL play and it is the least expensive pipeline in this entire deal.

Here is why the restriction works in your favor. When targeting cannot qualify people, the offer has to. A session that says "VA loan, and here is what you are leaving on the table" self-selects veterans harder than any targeting parameter would have. Wide reach plus a sharp offer plus organic proof beats precise targeting nobody is allowed to use anymore.

Now the part that matters more than the price. You mentioned a real estate agent working alongside you. If that agent and your lending arm share one marketing system, one audience, or one invoice, you have built the exact arrangement RESPA Section 8 exists to scrutinize, and the CFPB has issued consent orders against both the mortgage company and the real estate company in these cases. Separately, Meta has prohibited shared customer-list audiences across different business portfolios for housing and credit ads since March 2025, so it is against platform policy before it is ever a legal question. Two different authorities, same answer

How I structure it

  • Separate sub-account per party
  • Separate ad account and separate audiences
  • Separate invoice to each party
  • Flat fees that map to work actually performed
  • Each party pays only for what was done for them

Who owns the leads

  • Leads belong to whoever paid for the campaign that produced them. Always.
  • They land in that party's sub-account and nowhere else
  • No lead crosses from the lender's spend to the agent, or the reverse
  • That is not just a business rule. A lead routed from one settlement service provider to another is a referral, and referrals are what Section 8 is about
  • If both parties want leads, both parties fund their own campaigns

What I will not do

  • One shared account across lender and agent
  • Route leads paid for by one party to the other
  • One bill split by handshake
  • A fee that moves with referral volume
  • Any arrangement where one party subsidizes the other's marketing
  • Shared customer lists across business portfolios

Flat pricing is deliberate. A fee that scales with your ad budget is harder to defend as fair market value than a fee that maps to the work performed. This is structuring, not legal advice, and each party should have its own counsel review the arrangement before it starts.
